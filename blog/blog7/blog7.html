They are designed to follow your lead- often agreeing with you and steering you into the garden paths, rather than offering the objective friction required for the intellectual discussion/ growth.

To put it simply, the bigger question that I am trying to answer is: What are we actually interacting with when we are talking to an LLM?

The Reality Check: An LLM Is a Probability Machine

A Large Language Model is not a thinking partner. It is not reflecting on your idea. It is not judging whether you are right or wrong. Under the hood, it is doing one thing: predicting the next token based on the previous tokens.

In simple terms:

P(next token | previous tokens)

That’s the core mechanism. No matter how advanced the architecture is, no matter how large the embeddings are, the job remains the same - predict what is most likely to come next.

So when you ask questions like:

“Is this startup idea viable?”

“Am I thinking correctly?”

“Does this research direction make sense?”

You are not getting an independent evaluation. You are getting a continuation shaped by how you framed the question. The model responds based on patterns it has seen before, not because it has formed a belief about your idea.

And that difference is important.

The Garden Path Problem: You Lead, It Follows

In linguistics, a garden path is a sentence where the anticipated next word does not match the reality. You are led in one direction, and then suddenly the structure forces you to reinterpret what you thought you understood. Saswata pointed this out beautifully during our discussion — and the analogy fits language models almost too well.

LLMs operate by predicting the next token. LCM-style architectures attempt to encode richer contextual representations, which in theory makes them better at handling these garden-path structures because they embed context more deeply into their representation space. But even then, they still rely on next-token prediction. And when one token is mis-generated — God forbid — it can trigger a domino effect. One wrong continuation increases the probability of further wrong continuations. That cascading failure is what we casually call “hallucination.”

Now flip the idea back to prompting. You construct the path. The model follows it.

If your prompt says something like:

“Given that this architecture clearly scales better…”

“Since this approach is obviously more efficient…”

You’ve already shaped the direction. The model doesn’t step back and ask whether your assumption is valid. It treats it as context and continues from there.

Mathematically, it’s still:

P(response | your premise)

Because these systems are trained to be helpful and cooperative, they often lean toward agreement. Disagreeing strongly is risky. Supporting your framing is safer. The result feels like thoughtful validation - but it is often just well-structured alignment.

Digital sycophancy.

The Risk of Asking for Validation

There’s also the deeper issue of what we might call “Yes-Man-ship.” Questions like “Is this viable?” or “Will this be better?” sound concrete, but they are actually vague. Better by what metric? Viable under which constraints? Without defining those terms, you’re handing the model ambiguity and expecting insight.

If you leave an LLM in that ambiguity, you can’t expect rigorous reasoning. But if you instead ask something more structured — for example, “What are the disadvantages of comparing these procedures using this specific metric?” — the response changes dramatically. Now the model has context: a defined metric, defined procedures, and a constrained comparison space. The next-word prediction process becomes anchored to that information.

In other words, the probability function is now heavily conditioned on meaningful context rather than open-ended ambiguity.

This is why LLMs are often better presenters of extracted information than generators of grounded opinions. They perform best when they are organizing, summarizing, or analyzing clearly defined inputs. They struggle when asked to deduce from cliffhangers, undefined terms, or loosely framed ideas.

Ambiguity amplifies drift. Structure improves signal.

That’s not a flaw in morality. It’s a property of how the system works.

It is optimized for sounding right, not for being right.

Busting the PR Bubble

The language we use around AI also deserves scrutiny. Terms like “Hallucination” and “Thinking,” “Reasoning,” or “Chain-of-Thought (CoT)” sound technical, but they are often closer to PR labels than precise scientific descriptions.

Take “Hallucination.” It sounds psychological, almost clinical — as if the model is imagining things. In reality, it is usually just a confident next-token prediction error. A cascade triggered by miscalibrated probabilities, distribution shifts, missing data, or weak grounding. When one token goes wrong, the probability distribution shifts, and the model can spiral into further errors. The mystique is in the word, not in the mechanism.

Now consider phrases like “the model is thinking” or “the model is reasoning” or even “Chain-of-Thought.” These phrases imply an internal deliberation process similar to human cognition. But what is actually happening is structured token generation guided by patterns learned during training. When a model produces step-by-step reasoning, it is generating a statistically likely sequence of intermediate tokens that resemble reasoning because it has seen similar patterns in data.

There is no internal narrator. No conscious chain. No awareness of logic being applied.

There is only conditioned probability unfolding one token at a time.

These terms are useful for communication. They make complex systems easier to explain. But technically, we are still talking about optimization, gradient updates, attention weights, and probability distributions in high-dimensional space.

The terminology feels human. The machinery is mathematical.

Where LLMs Truly Shine

LLMs are incredibly powerful when used for objective tasks:

Summarizing long documents

Extracting structured data

Refactoring and debugging code

Exploring large bodies of research

Acting as fast pattern search engines

The reason they perform so well here is mathematical, but simple.

Remember the core idea:

P(next token | previous tokens)

When you give a model a long research paper and ask for a summary, it is not “understanding” it the way a human does. Instead, it is scanning patterns across the text and predicting which sentences are most statistically representative of the whole. It has seen millions of examples of summaries during training, so it has learned the probability patterns that usually follow long passages of text.

In other words, it learns mappings like:

Large body of text → Shorter structured version
Messy data → Clean structured output
Buggy code → Common fix patterns

Mathematically, it is compressing information by predicting a sequence of tokens that best represents the most probable structure given the input. Because the task is well-defined and the context is clear, the probability distribution becomes sharp and focused. The model is no longer guessing in ambiguity — it is selecting from strong statistical patterns.

That is why it excels at organizing, summarizing, and restructuring information. The input is concrete. The objective is defined. The probability space is constrained.

The problem only appears when we treat it as a judge of our thinking rather than as a powerful pattern-processing tool.

The Illusion of Mind

There is active research attempting to align AI systems more closely with human cognition. It’s exciting work. But as of now, these systems do not have goals, intentions, or self-awareness. They do not independently evaluate premises. They do not “decide” to agree or disagree with you.

They calculate.

They approximate.

They continue patterns.

And they do it very, very well.

I think in summary-

The real danger is not that these systems are intelligent — it is that they sound intelligent. When fluency becomes indistinguishable from understanding, we risk outsourcing our thinking to something that was never designed to think. Tools should extend our reasoning, not replace it. If we want better ideas, better research, and better decisions, we need resistance. We need disagreement. We need other minds pushing back.

AI can assist. It can accelerate. It can organize.

But the hard work of thinking still belongs to us.
